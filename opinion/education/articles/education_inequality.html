Education and Inequality

A reflection on a conversation I had.

CuriousPanda • 3 min read

I was recently at the MOE’s Special Awards Ceremony 2026 and…I was bored. Naturally, I turned to my side and struck up a conversation that somehow turned into a discussion on education inequality. In an event meant to celebrate the pinnacle of students’ achievements, we can’t help but wonder—does our education system give everyone a fair chance of receiving this honour? And what achievements of our students are what actually celebrating?

Fundamentally, Singapore’s education system relies heavily on the idea of meritocracy which is the belief that students who put in the effort and excel should receive more resources. While this sounds compelling on paper, it is deeply unfair in practice. And it is all due to one reason--Parentocracy. Parentocracy is the phenomenon whereby the social-economic status of your family heavily influences your educational success. If you are a STEM student like me obsessed with concrete data, the Singapore University of Social Sciences have published a paper which underscored the correlation between one’s social-economic status and PSLE grades. Unfortunately, one’s education journey could arguably be modelled by an exponential curve—the headstart given by one’s background will lead to a disproportionately larger likelihood for success in their education. This is the sad reality that we Singaporeans face today.

However, I realise I have yet to even define the term success up to this point. Inequality need not be between individuals of different socioeconomic classes, but between different talent pipelines. In recent years, there have been multiple calls to “broaden the definition of success” and “recognise different types of excellence”. Interestingly, just as I have done for 2 paragraphs thus far, Singapore too has danced with the definition of success for years. And understandably so—we have to realise that our policy makers are living life on the edge by carefully balancing between Singapore’s survival narrative and creating a more inclusive society. As such, we often see policies such as the substitution of the PSLE aggregate score for a T-score that is ultimately applicable solely for superficial changes.

To understand why, we have to discuss Singapore’s economic model. At its core, Singapore is a knowledge-intensive economy that utilises rich human capital to attract Multi-National Corporations (MNCs) to invest in Singapore and create employment opportunities. The abundant inflow of investments is what allows Singapore to boast one of the largest income per capita in the entire world. If so, then the preservation of such valuable human capital is of paramount importance to our prosperity. How have we produced such results? You probably already guessed the answer—it is through our “toxic” education system that produced a highly-educated workforce. It is then no surprise that the government is not incentivised to make drastic changes to our education system.

Of course, some may argue that the government has been actively encouraging alternative paths to success such as Direct School Admissions (DSA) for sports and music. However, such decisions may arguably be driven by economic reasons as well. In Singapore, if you are talented in sports, there is little opportunities for you to succeed professionally. Singapore simply does not have a sufficiently large audience base to generate the revenue required to support professional athletes. If so, what occupations could they possibly take up when they are deeply skilled in their sport? Coaches—and the demand for coaches can be artificially increased by programmes such as DSA that suddenly places tangible value on the sports and arts. Ultimately, my argument is not that Singapore is neglecting diverse talents, but rather that inclusivity remains subservient to pragmatic economic drivers.

Does this mean that Singapore’s education system will remain centred around training knowledge-intensive workers? Not quite. I have good reasons to believe that entrepreneurship, which has been proven as a powerful alternative to a traditional career and innovative driver of the economy by the United States, could thrive in Singapore. Considering the potential for entrepreneurship to drive our economy, the government has intentionally created an environment for entrepreneurship to thrive such as lowering legal and financial barriers. For instance, small-medium enterprises (SMEs) can easily apply for loans from the government to ease their cash flow. Moreover, the younger generation of Singaporeans are having a paradigm shift in mindset from the conventional white-collar jobs to bold business ventures that are better suited for their talents and aspirations.

Finally, let’s return to our question on education inequality. For many years, we have identified the flaws in our system yet little has been done to fix them at a fundamental level. The reason is simple--Singapore’s education system exists for the sake of economic prosperity that has uplifted our predecessors from poverty and has allowed many generations to enjoy a high standard of living. So long as this premise for education has not changed and no convincingly better alternative is in sight, it would be challenging to justify a drastic change in education policies. Perhaps the wiser approach would be to invest our efforts into making our education system more sustainable for everyone and provide multiple entry points for late-bloomers who struggle to scale the exponential curve.

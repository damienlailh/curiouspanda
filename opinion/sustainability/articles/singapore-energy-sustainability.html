How can Singapore Truly Achieve Sustainability in Energy?

A Singaporean's take on the future of our energy.

CuriousPanda • 5 min read

Singapore’s biggest strength is not talent. It is stability. China outperforms us in the International Olympiads and practically every other metric for measuring education standards. Yet, Chinese billionaires want to move to Singapore and relocate their businesses here. When MNCs wish to open their headquarters, Singapore is often their first choice. What is it about this tiny country that attracts them?

At its core, I believe it is stability—and stability necessitates sustainability. To be sustainable is to use resources without the need for borrowing them from the future. At first glance, this may seem more like a concern for the environment rather than strengthening Singapore’s stability. Yet, it dictates the country’s independent function from external influences. For instance, Singapore’s main source of energy is imported natural gas—around 95% of our energy comes from it. It is then naïve to claim to be unaffected by these exporters when almost all our energy originates from them. True stability, then, depends on our ability to control our own energy resources. In this issue, I shall discuss my thoughts on Singapore achieving energy sustainability.

I would first love to set some contexts. Sustainability is multifaceted—from energy to food, water to ecosystems, there are many areas which deserve our attention. Why am I specifically discussing energy? Because our civilization runs on energy. In fact, energy is so fundamental that physicists categorise the level of civilisation progress based on the type and amount of energy they are able to harness. Energy is also the basis for other securities—if we want to have a steady water supply, desalination and NEWater are the key sources for Singapore. These are not difficult to implement, but costly in energy usage. If we are able to make energy cheaper and more readily available, it would be arguably the most significant step forward for water security.

The US-Iran war has reminded countries of how vulnerable energy supply chains are. Oil prices have shot up to over $100 a barrel, and many countries are anticipating elevated levels of inflation due to the rise in cost of energy production. Such volatility and Singapore’s susceptibility to these vagaries directly undermines our core strength as a safe haven and stable hub amidst the raging storms. Singapore’s key industries such as advanced manufacturing of semiconductors are energy-intensive—should these industries slow down due to energy instability, investor confidence would wane and Singapore’s prized reputation would be threatened.

In light of this reality, Singapore has focused on two main strategies: diversification of imports and storage of excess supply. In essence, Singapore trying to emulate the internet structure for its imports—with numerous independent sources, no one disruption is capable of paralysing the entire ecosystem. For instance, Singapore signed additional trade deals with New Zealand to ensure mutual flows of necessities including energy. However, in this era of geopolitical instability, it is extremely precarious for your future to be dependent on such tenuous trust agreements. We have seen countless times how larger and more powerful countries such as the United States have violated mutual agreements without any notable consequences.

Personally, I believe the diversification of imports represents the foundation step of a multi-step framework that the government will eventually actualise. By increasing the imports of energy such as electricity from different countries such as Australia, Singapore is actively building up infrastructure such as submarine power cables—while it appears to be the medium for purchasing electricity, I believe there lies a deeper consideration. Once the infrastructure is developed, Singapore can actively invest and own companies that run solar farms in Australia who then sell electricity back to Singapore. By offshoring the production process to a country where land is cheaper, Singapore is able to withhold a greater level of control without compromising on its own limited land.

Next, Singapore also requires substantial storage mechanisms in times of uncertainty. So long as the generation process is located in another country, Singapore will need redundancies to fall back on. For example, Singapore has months of crude oil and natural gas reserves below Jurong Islands for our energy needs. This allows us to tide over any near-term shortages and provide buffer as we devise solutions to unforeseen circumstances. However, storage methods do not have to be only imported raw materials! Singapore can look into efficient methods of converting its energy into dense forms of energy. For the longest time, hydrogen has been of keen interest. In my mind, as battery technologies have been exponentially advancing, we may see a stage where huge solar farms in Australia charge the small battery farms in Singapore. At this very moment, the main issue is scale and ability to store massive amounts of energy for our daily operations. Ultimately, it truly depends on the maturation of the technology which I have faith will give Singapore a competitive edge if we do indeed become proficient at it (the future will be dominated by chips and batteries. Our AM industry focuses only on the former, and not the latter).

Thus far, Singapore has only managed to mitigate volatility, but remains far from able to fully decouple from the other countries. In order to do so, another technology that used to be a taboo for Singapore is slowly gaining attention—nuclear energy. Small modular reactors (SMR) implemented in the United States of America have already progressed through their proof-of-concept phase and are particularly suitable to implement at a small scale in Singapore. In fact, if one follows the news, we can start to see Singapore taking subtle but significant steps towards implementing nuclear energy. For instance, Singapore signed a five-year cooperation framework with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) for 2026 to 2031 to strengthen nuclear capabilities. It is also not uncommon to see our ministers dropping “casual” remarks on their consideration of nuclear energy in Singapore’s near future.

So, we have looked at both the current and future measures that Singapore aims to take in order to develop our energy security. Are there other potential solutions? Yes! Disruptive technologies are developing at unprecedented rates every passing moment—AI is the quintessential example. As such, it would be unsurprising if previously unsuitable technologies such as geothermal would undergo a breakthrough and become effective for Singapore. Yet, this solely remains hypothetical and we should not be dependent on such slim chances. Ultimately, while I believe Singapore’s government tries to plan 20 years ahead, they can only do so with all the current information at hand. They are not oracles. As such, I believe that the above discussion is the most likely blueprint that they will follow as we navigate Singapore’s energy landscape in the next 10-20 years.

Are all these sufficiently comprehensive and rigorous? Yes and no—I emphasised on the need for Singapore to be self-reliant as a central theme. However, as much as I see the pressing urgency and value in doing so, I must admit that there will inevitably be some degree of external reliance. Singapore can build SMRs to generate their own electricity—but there will still be a need to import Uranium fuel. It almost seems as if we will never be able to escape from the reality of external dependence and Singapore is doomed to be susceptible to the ever-changing world dynamics. As a small player on the world poker table, becoming more self-reliant shows less cards to others. But it doesn’t deny the fact that we still have to play with the other players. Perhaps true stability is not mindlessly aiming for total independence, but having a balance of mutual collaboration while still hiding some cards for the crucial moments.
